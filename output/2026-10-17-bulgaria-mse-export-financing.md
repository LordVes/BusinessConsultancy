# Financing of Export-Oriented Medium-Sized Enterprises in Bulgaria

- Date: 2026-10-17
- Audience: Potential VPs, directors, management boards and owners with an interest in managing medium-sized enterprises (MSEs)
- Scope: Export-oriented MSEs (EU definition: 50-249 employees, turnover up to EUR 50M or balance sheet up to EUR 43M) in manufacturing and IT/outsourcing; last 3-5 years, latest data to October 2026
- Status: **Draft with material evidence gaps.** Read "Scope and methodology" before relying on any figure.

## Executive summary

- Bank debt is the dominant external financing channel. Bulgarian capital markets are not a realistic source for most MSEs: the BSE recorded no IPOs, bond offers or warrant issues in 2025 [5].
- Banking is concentrated. The five largest banks hold 76.3% of system assets at end-2025: UBB 19.6%, DSK 18.9%, UniCredit Bulbank 18.1%, Eurobank Bulgaria 11.9%, Fibank 7.8% [4]. These are asset shares, not corporate-loan shares.
- Credit conditions improved in 2025 (corporate credit growth, falling average rates), and euro adoption on 1 January 2026 removes lev/euro conversion cost and currency risk for exporters. Evidence of measured post-euro effects on MSE financing cost is **not yet available** in what I could access [8][9].
- Public and EU guarantees (Bulgarian Development Bank, National Guarantee Fund, EIB/EIF, InvestEU) are the main way MSEs get past collateral limits. About 58% of NGF-guaranteed loans needed no collateral, and a dedicated BDB export working-capital product launched in November 2024 [3].
- **I could not find hard data on three things you asked for:** the maturity split of MSE or export-firm debt, the use of family funds and bootstrapping, and bank shares in MSE or export lending. They are stated as gaps below, not estimated.
- Management implication (judgement, not a sourced finding): MSEs relying on short-term working-capital lines for long-lived investments, with one dominant bank and little non-bank financing, carry avoidable refinancing risk.

## Scope and methodology (due diligence approach)

**Approach used**
- Source ranking: official and institutional sources first (BNB, ECB, EIB, BDB, BSE, Commission); bank and association publications second; trade press and commentary only for context.
- Every figure is tagged with publisher, date and a link in the Sources list. Facts, estimates and opinion are kept separate.
- Cross-checking: where two sources disagree, both figures are shown and the conflict is flagged rather than resolved by assumption.
- Proxy rule: where MSE-specific data does not exist, I use SME-wide or corporate-wide data and say so.

**Limitations (important)**
- **Primary documents not opened.** Network restrictions in this working environment blocked direct access to bnb.bg, the Association of Banks in Bulgaria (abanksb.bg) and economic.bg. Figures below come from web search summaries of those and other sources, not from the original tables. Every number attributed to the BNB or ABB should be checked against the original before external use.
- **No MSE- or exporter-specific series.** Bulgarian statistics reviewed group enterprises by size band or sector in aggregate. No source gave the financing mix, maturity or bank shares for export-oriented medium-sized firms specifically.
- **Maturity data not retrieved.** The BNB publishes corporate loan volumes by original maturity (up to 1 year, 1-5 years, over 5 years) but I could not read the 2025 figures.
- **Mixed dates.** Several items (leasing, some programmes) are older or undated, and some 2026 items come from secondary sources.
- **No interviews or company-level data** were used.

## Key findings

### 1. Financing mix

- Bank loans are the main external source; no source reviewed gave a measured MSE financing mix (debt, equity, retained earnings, family funds). **Gap.**
- Euro-area survey evidence (not Bulgaria-specific) shows the share of firms applying for bank loans fell, mainly due to high levels of internal funds; SME external financing for fixed investment fell to 28% from 31% [10]. Whether this holds for Bulgarian exporters is unverified.
- Family and owner bootstrapping: **no data found.** A survey cited in secondary press names lack of capital as an obstacle for 45% of internationalizing firms [12]; the sample and date were not verified.
- Working capital dominates guaranteed lending: 56% (BGN 762M) of guaranteed financing in the NGF programme was working-capital loans [3].

### 2. Maturity of financing

- Direct maturity split for MSEs: **not obtained (gap).**
- Pricing signal for long loans: new loans to enterprises over 5 years in lev carried about 3.7% in December 2025 (CEIC series, secondary) [2].
- Euro adoption: lev loans became euro loans on 1 January 2026 at the fixed rate, with unchanged rates and instalments; loans tied to the BNB base rate moved to EURIBOR-based pricing (3-month for non-revolving, 1-month for revolving, per UniCredit Bulbank) [9]. Floating-rate exposure is therefore now to EURIBOR.
- Non-bank lenders show a long-dated book (47.4% over five years) but this is not the bank-loan population relevant to MSEs [2].

### 3. Capital markets and non-bank channels

- **Bulgarian Stock Exchange / BEAM (SME growth market):** no IPOs, bond offers or warrant issues in 2025, versus four IPOs, four bond offers and one warrant issue the year before. Cumulatively about 20 IPOs and over EUR 52M raised; 19 stock issues and 7 bond issues trade on BEAM [5].
- Prospectus exemption threshold raised from EUR 3M to EUR 8M (2022) and, per one report, to EUR 12M from 7 August 2026 [5] (verify against FSC).
- Example issue size: ELG raised EUR 5M on BEAM in 2024 [5]. This indicates a ticket size far below typical MSE investment needs.
- **Private equity and venture capital:** activity exists but is not focused on export MSEs in the sources found. Examples: Advent/TBI Bank (EUR 300M), Blackstone/Sunotec (EUR 250M, battery storage), the EUR 32M Bulgarian Innovation Capital fund under the Fund of Funds, and Vitosha Venture Partners (EUR 26M) [6]. Chambers reports PE investors are now the most consistent participants in Bulgarian M&A [6].
- **Leasing, factoring, trade finance:** **no current data found.** Only older material (2008-2016) on EBRD and BDB credit lines to leasing companies [7].

### 4. Banks most heavily involved

- Concentration at end-2025 (share of system assets): UBB 19.60%, DSK Bank 18.93%, UniCredit Bulbank 18.07%, Eurobank Bulgaria 11.88%, Fibank 7.82%; top five 76.3% [4].
- Lending growth: system-wide, mortgages grew +28.9% year on year and corporate loans +3.9% (+BGN 2.0bn) in the Q4 2025 review [4]. Note this conflicts in basis with BNB statements of 9.3% credit growth to non-financial corporations at end-May 2025 and 15.5% for corporations and households combined at end-2025 [1][2]; definitions differ and were not reconciled.
- UBB emerged as the largest lender after the merger with KBC Bank Bulgaria and expanded corporate and household lending faster than the market [4].
- Development and guarantee support runs through commercial banks: ProCredit Bank Bulgaria (EIB guarantee, about EUR 150M) [3], Allianz Bank Bulgaria (reported EUR 190M guarantee, March 2026, unverified) [3], UniCredit Bulbank (InvestEU, EUR 100M with BDB) and DSK Bank (InvestEU guarantee, over EUR 300M) [1].
- The Bulgarian Development Bank (BDB) increased its asset share from 1.53% to 3.03% after a state cash injection [4].
- Shares in MSE or export lending specifically: **not found.**

### 5. Public support and EU instruments

- BDB's National Guarantee Fund: over a three-year cycle about 5,900 loans to 5,200 companies, BGN 1.36bn; nearly 58% of loans needed no collateral [3]. A separate 2024 figure of over 4,100 loans for BGN 1.7bn was also reported and appears inconsistent with the cycle total; treat both as unverified [3].
- BDB export product (launched November 2024): working-capital loans for firms with at least three completed financial years, positive equity and an export strategy [3].
- Secondary reporting says EU programmes for internationalization remain largely unused due to limited information and application capacity [12].

## Risks, uncertainties and open questions

**Risks and challenges for export MSEs**
- **Concentration and dependence on a few banks:** five banks hold 76.3% of assets; an MSE with a single main bank has limited bargaining power [4].
- **Collateral and credit-history barriers:** secondary evidence says banks view export-minded firms as riskier, with higher rates and stricter collateral [12]. Guarantees reduce but do not remove this.
- **Maturity mismatch (hypothesis):** guaranteed lending is weighted to working capital [3]; if the same pattern holds across MSEs, long-lived investments may be financed with short-term debt. Untested.
- **Capital market access:** effectively closed for most MSEs in 2025; deal sizes are small [5].
- **Rate and EURIBOR exposure** after euro adoption for floating-rate borrowers [9].
- **Export demand and cost of disputes:** a cited survey puts the cost of resolving foreign trade disputes as the top obstacle (50%) [12], unverified.
- **Credit quality:** NPL ratio for corporations 4.53% in Q2 2025 [1].
- **Underused EU funds** due to administrative capacity [12].

**Open questions**
- What is the actual debt/equity/internal-funds mix for Bulgarian export MSEs?
- What share of MSE debt is short-term, and how much is refinanced annually?
- Did financing costs for exporters fall after euro adoption, as in Estonia (about 0.8 percentage points per Ebury analysis, secondary) [8]?
- Which banks lead in export and MSE lending by loan volume?

## Key takeaways and recommended next steps

### Key takeaways

- Bank debt, often supported by public guarantees, is the realistic core of MSE financing; capital markets are a marginal option today.
- Banking concentration and collateral requirements are the main structural constraints; guarantee schemes are the main relief.
- Euro adoption lowers exporter currency risk; the effect on borrowing cost is expected but not yet evidenced.
- Evidence on exporter-specific maturity, mix and bank shares is missing in public sources reviewed. Decisions should not rely on this draft alone.

### Recommended next steps

1. **Close the data gaps (highest priority):** open the primary BNB tables (loans by maturity and sector, interest rates, "Banks in Bulgaria"), NSI and Eurostat SME-by-size data, and the ECB SAFE Bulgaria country tables. To enable this from here, allow bnb.bg, abanksb.bg, economic.bg and similar domains in the environment's network settings, or send me the files.
2. **Ground-truth with practitioners:** short interviews with 5-10 CFOs/owners of export MSEs and 2-3 relationship managers (including at UBB, DSK, UniCredit Bulbank and BDB) to test the maturity-mismatch and collateral hypotheses.
3. **For management teams now (judgement):**
   - Review the maturity profile of debt against asset life and cash-flow timing.
   - Ask your banks about NGF/InvestEU guarantee eligibility and the BDB export product to cut collateral needs.
   - Compare at least two banks on EURIBOR-based pricing after the lev conversion.
   - Treat BEAM as an option only for issues in the low single-digit EUR millions.
4. **Follow-up report option:** a second pass on leasing, factoring and trade finance once current data is available.
