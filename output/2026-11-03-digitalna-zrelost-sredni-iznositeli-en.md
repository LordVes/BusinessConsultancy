## Summary

- **38.3% of Bulgarian SMEs** (10–249 employees) reached at least a basic level of digital intensity in 2025, against 71.4% in the EU. This is among the lowest levels in the EU and below the trajectory in Bulgaria's national roadmap. [1, 9]

- **On security, Bulgaria ranks last in the EU:** 31.3% of Bulgarian enterprises apply at least 5 of 11 cybersecurity measures, against 56.9% in the EU, the lowest share in the EU (2024). [9]

- **Five hypotheses** are set out for testing. There are no survey results yet.

## 1. Why this matters for exporters

This whitepaper is for owners and managers of Bulgarian firms with 30–150 employees that export. For them, digital maturity directly shapes how they work with foreign customers: how they take orders, issue documents, trace batches and prove that their data is protected.

The group matters for exports. In 2024, 899 Bulgarian manufacturers with 50–249 employees exported goods, or 28% of all exporting manufacturers. They accounted for 18.0% of the value of manufactured goods exports; 77.1% came from enterprises with 250 or more employees. [8] These figures show the number of exporters and the value of exports, but not what share of a firm's turnover comes from exports. We found no such indicator by firm size.

The European Commission notes that low SME digital intensity may constrain productivity growth and the position of Bulgarian firms in higher-value segments of European value chains. [9]

### What they have in common: our reading

- All frameworks combine strategy and operations. Deloitte reports that focusing on only one of the two does not deliver the expected effect on profit and revenue. [14]

- Scales have 3 to 6 levels and describe a move from isolated initiatives to company-wide change. [12, 13, 14, 15]

- KPMG separates two perspectives: how deliberately digitalisation is managed and how widely it is used. [15] We borrow this idea.

- Consulting benchmarks are built mainly on large companies. For a 60-person firm, a comparison with "leaders" is less useful than a profile against its own targets.

## 4. An eight-dimension assessment approach

**MSP Compass's own approach.** It is built from the frameworks reviewed and adapted to firms with 30–150 employees. It has not yet been tested with a survey.

### Four principles

-
#### Start from the management decision
The assessment answers a specific question: for example, which investment to make first, or what the next large customer will ask for. Without such a question, nobody owns the result.

-
#### Report a profile, not one number
An average hides the bottleneck. Show the level for each dimension and its weakest criterion.

-
#### Targets follow strategy
Not every dimension needs to reach level 4. For most dimensions, level 3 is the realistic target; a higher level makes sense where it is a source of advantage.

-
#### End with a roadmap
The assessment ends with a 90-day plan and a 12-month roadmap with owners, not with a heat map.

### Four levels

Each of the 30 criteria has a description for every level that can be checked on site. The full list is in the appendix and in the self-assessment (in Bulgarian).

| Level | Name | What it looks like |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | Manual and ad hoc | Work depends on people, paper and separate files. Results are hard to repeat. |
| 2 | Digitised in islands | Systems exist, but in separate units. Data is moved between them by hand. |
| 3 | Integrated across functions | The process runs through connected systems without re-keying. Responsibilities are clear. |
| 4 | Data-driven and continuously improved | Decisions are made on data from the systems, and the process is measured and improved regularly. |

### Two perspectives

Each dimension also gets two questions: how deliberately it is managed and how widely it is used. A firm can have a well-managed project used in only one department, or the reverse. The two scores show where the problem lies.

| Level | Management: How deliberately is the dimension managed? | Pervasiveness: How widely is it used day to day? |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | Nobody is responsible for it. | Only individual people. |
| 2 | There is an owner, but no targets or budget. | One department or one site. |
| 3 | There is an owner, targets and budget; progress is reviewed regularly. | Most of the departments concerned. |
| 4 | Managed with indicators and adjusted at least quarterly. | Everywhere, including with customers and suppliers. |

## 5. The process: 4–6 weeks

- Week 1

### Decision and scope
State the management decision the assessment will support. Define the scope (sites, product lines, markets), the sponsor in management and the target level for each dimension.

- Weeks 1–3

### Evidence
6–8 interviews with managers and key users; an inventory of the systems and files in use; 2–3 on-site process walk-throughs, for example one export order from enquiry to payment; about 15 hard data points, such as the share of orders keyed in by hand, time from order to invoice, number of duplicates in master data, scrap, and days to payment.

- Week 4

### Scoring and calibration
Two people score each criterion independently from the evidence. Differences are resolved in a workshop with management: every score must have evidence behind it.

- Weeks 5–6

### Priorities and plan
Gaps against the targets are ranked by their effect on the management decision. The result is a 90-day plan and a 12-month roadmap.

## 6. Five pitfalls

- **Scoring without a decision.** The assessment is done "to know where we stand", and then nobody acts on it.

- **Vague level definitions.** Levels such as "partly" or "good" give different scores from different people. Each level must describe something you can see.

- **An average that hides the bottleneck.** An average of 2.6 can hide a level 1 in master data that holds everything else back.

- **Rewarding apps instead of data and ownership.** The number of systems installed is not maturity. Without an owner and clean data, a new system becomes one more island.

- **Stopping at the heat map.** The coloured map is an interim result. The value lies in choosing which 2–3 things to change first and who leads them.

## 8. Where requirements are heading

**Interpretation.** This section is our reading of the sources, not established fact.

### Data and AI foundations become a precondition

Fact: among firms with 50–249 employees, AI use in Bulgaria rose from 5.5% in 2023 to 13.3% in 2025, while in the EU it rose from 13.1% to 30.4%. [2] The European Commission recommends that Bulgaria accelerate the use of AI, cloud and data analytics among SMEs through more advisory support and skills. [9]

Interpretation: AI only pays off on top of orderly master data and connected systems. For most mid-sized firms the first step towards AI is probably the "Data and technology foundation" dimension, not a new tool.

### Security and data become buyer requirements

Fact: in February 2026 Bulgaria transposed the NIS2 Directive into its Cybersecurity Act. It covers 18 sectors, with stricter risk management and reporting requirements for medium and large businesses. [9] In a spring 2025 pulse survey by the Bulgarian Industrial Association, only 9.4% of firms considered themselves fully ready, and more than a third had not started preparing or were unaware of their obligations. [9]

Interpretation: customers within the scope of NIS2 will ask their suppliers for more evidence of security. For exporters, security questionnaires are likely to become part of the commercial conversation.

## 9. Hypotheses to test

**These are hypotheses, not findings.** There are no survey results yet. Each hypothesis comes with the available data and how we will test it.

-
Hypothesis 1

### Most firms have an ERP, but master data is weak and the export interface runs on Excel.

**What we know.** Eurostat data puts the first part in doubt: only 41.9% of Bulgarian firms with 50–249 employees report using an ERP (2025). The hypothesis may hold for exporters but not for the average firm. [5]

**How we will test it.** A systems inventory and a walk-through of one export order: the number of manual hand-offs and of files outside the ERP.

-
Hypothesis 2

### The binding constraint is people and ownership, not technology spend.

**What we know.** In a 2025 survey of 847 firms by the Bulgarian Industrial Association, 40% named a lack of digital skills as the main obstacle, and 33% named financial constraints. [9]

**How we will test it.** Scores on the people and ownership criteria compared with the technology criteria; a check whether every project has an owner with time set aside.

-
Hypothesis 3

### Production data capture lags behind the digitisation of sales and finance.

**What we know.** There is no current national data on shop-floor digitalisation, so this is a gap in the evidence, not a finding.

**How we will test it.** Comparing the scores for "Production and operations" with those for "Customers" and "Order-to-cash" within the same firm.

-
Hypothesis 4

### Security readiness is below what key foreign customers will soon ask for.

**What we know.** In 2024, 31.3% of Bulgarian enterprises applied at least 5 of 11 security measures, against 56.9% in the EU. In a spring 2025 pulse survey by the Bulgarian Industrial Association, only 9.4% of firms considered themselves fully ready for NIS2. [9]

**How we will test it.** Comparing the measures in place with the security questionnaires received from customers in the last 12 months.

-
Hypothesis 5

### Funding programmes are used for tools rather than process change.

**What we know.** The European Commission notes that support measures have not yet led to large-scale change in SME digital maturity. [9]

**How we will test it.** A review of funded projects: do they include a process change, an owner and a measurable benefit, or only the purchase of software and hardware?

## Appendix: 30 criteria with level definitions

#### 1. Strategy and leadership 4 criteria

**S1 · Digital goals tied to a business decision**

- 1No written digitalisation goals; projects start on individual initiative.
- 2A list of projects by department, not linked to business goals.
- 33–5 digital goals are derived from the business strategy, each with an owner and a deadline.
- 4Goals are reviewed at least quarterly against measured results and adjusted.

**S2 · Leadership involvement**

- 1Digitalisation is "IT's job" or an outside firm's.
- 2The owner approves purchases but does not follow up on delivery.
- 3A member of management owns the digital roadmap and reports on it regularly.
- 4Management decides on data from the systems, not on one-off Excel reports.

**S3 · Roadmap and priorities**

- 1No plan; purchases react to a problem or an offer.
- 2A plan for the next purchase, but no sequence of steps.
- 3A 12-month roadmap with priorities, dependencies and budget.
- 4The roadmap is updated at least yearly against results and market changes.

**S4 · Key customer requirements**

- 1We do not know what digital requirements our foreign customers have.
- 2We react when a customer sets a requirement.
- 3Key customers' requirements (formats, portals, security) are documented and built into the plan.
- 4We anticipate requirements and use them as an advantage in negotiations.

#### 2. Customers and markets 4 criteria

**C1 · Customer data**

- 1Contacts and history live in emails and salespeople's personal notes.
- 2There is a CRM or shared spreadsheet, but it is filled in patchily.
- 3All salespeople work in one CRM linked to quotes and orders.
- 4CRM data is used to forecast sales and prioritise customers.

**C2 · Quotes and pricing**

- 1Every quote is built by hand in Word or Excel.
- 2Templates exist, but prices and stock are checked by hand.
- 3Quotes are generated by a system with current prices, stock and lead times.
- 4The margin per quote is visible upfront, and win/loss is analysed.

**C3 · Digital channels to foreign customers**

- 1Communication is by email and phone only.
- 2There is a website and catalogue, but no electronic ordering.
- 3Key customers order or track orders electronically (portal, EDI or B2B shop).
- 4Digital channels carry a measurable share of sales and are developed using customer behaviour data.

**C4 · Complaints and feedback**

- 1Complaints are kept on paper or in emails.
- 2Complaints are logged in a spreadsheet, with no root-cause analysis.
- 3Complaints are logged in a system linked to the order and batch.
- 4Root causes are analysed regularly and lead to product or process changes.

#### 3. Order-to-cash and supply chain 4 criteria

**O1 · Order processing**

- 1Orders are keyed in by hand from email or PDF, often more than once.
- 2The order is entered once in the ERP, but confirmations and documents are made by hand.
- 3The order runs from intake to invoice in one system without re-keying.
- 4Time and errors in the order-to-cash cycle are measured and deliberately reduced.

**O2 · Export documents and invoicing**

- 1Invoices, CMRs and accompanying documents are prepared by hand in Excel or Word.
- 2Invoices come from the system, but export documents are made separately.
- 3Invoices and export documents are generated by one system and sent electronically.
- 4Documents are exchanged electronically with customers, forwarders and banks, with automatic checks.

**O3 · Purchasing and inventory**

- 1Supplier orders are placed from experience and stock checked by eye.
- 2Stock is kept in a system, but planning is done in Excel.
- 3Material needs are calculated by the system from orders and the production plan.
- 4Stock and supplier lead times are optimised with data and scenarios.

**O4 · Shipment tracking**

- 1Shipment status comes from a phone call to the forwarder.
- 2Status is checked separately on each forwarder's portal.
- 3Shipment status is visible in our own system and shared with the customer.
- 4Delays are predicted and the customer is notified automatically.

#### 4. Production and operations 4 criteria

**P1 · Production planning**

- 1The plan is on a whiteboard or in the shift supervisor's head.
- 2The plan is in Excel and updated by hand.
- 3The plan is made in a system linked to orders and stock.
- 4The plan recalculates on change and shows the effect on customer due dates.

**P2 · Production reporting**

- 1Output is recorded on paper at the end of the shift.
- 2Data is entered by hand into a spreadsheet or system after the shift.
- 3Quantity, scrap and downtime are recorded at the workstation as they happen.
- 4Machine data flows in automatically and is used to analyse effectiveness (for example OEE).

**P3 · Quality and batch traceability**

- 1Quality is checked visually; records are on paper.
- 2Inspection results are in spreadsheets, not linked to the batch.
- 3Every batch can be traced in the system from raw material to customer.
- 4Quality data is analysed statistically to prevent defects.

**P4 · Equipment maintenance**

- 1We repair things when they break.
- 2There is a preventive maintenance schedule in a spreadsheet.
- 3Maintenance is planned and logged in a system with history per machine.
- 4Maintenance is planned from machine condition data.

#### 5. Data and technology foundation 4 criteria

**D1 · Master data**

- 1Items, customers and suppliers are repeated across files with different codes.
- 2There is a master register in the ERP, but with duplicates and incomplete fields.
- 3There is one source of master data and rules for who creates and maintains it.
- 4Master data quality is measured and has an owner with time set aside for it.

**D2 · System integration**

- 1Systems are not connected; data is moved by hand.
- 2Data is transferred by exporting and importing files.
- 3Core systems (ERP, CRM, warehouse, production) exchange data automatically.
- 4New applications connect in a standard way (API), with no manual steps.

**D3 · Reporting and analysis**

- 1Reports are built by hand in Excel when needed.
- 2Standard reports exist, but figures differ between departments.
- 3Key indicators are visible on a shared dashboard from one data source.
- 4Data analysis, including forecasts, is part of routine decisions.

**D4 · Infrastructure and cloud**

- 1A server in the office, with no clear support or backups.
- 2Some services are in the cloud, with no overall policy.
- 3It is clear what runs in the cloud and what on-premises, with contracted support and backups.
- 4Infrastructure is monitored and scaled, and costs are tracked per service.

#### 6. People and organisation 3 criteria

**H1 · Digital skills**

- 1Skills depend on individuals; there is no training.
- 2Training happens only when a new system is introduced.
- 3There is a skills plan by role and regular training.
- 4Skills are assessed, and key users train their colleagues.

**H2 · Ownership of processes and data**

- 1Nobody owns a process end to end.
- 2Responsibility sits with departments; problems between departments have no owner.
- 3Every core process and every master data set has a named owner.
- 4Owners manage their processes with indicators and initiate improvements themselves.

**H3 · Capacity for change**

- 1No one inside the firm can lead digital projects.
- 2Projects are led by the software vendor.
- 3There is an internal project lead or IT owner with time set aside.
- 4A team leads changes consistently and measures the effect.

#### 7. Risk, security and compliance 4 criteria

**R1 · Basic cybersecurity measures**

- 1Antivirus and passwords, nothing more.
- 2Some measures are in place (for example backups), but not systematically.
- 3Multi-factor authentication, regular backups, updates and staff training are in place.
- 4Measures are tested regularly, for example with a penetration test and a restore test.

**R2 · Customer and regulatory requirements**

- 1We do not know whether NIS2 or our customers' requirements apply to us.
- 2We answer security questionnaires when they arrive, without evidence.
- 3We have documented policies and evidence we can show a customer or auditor.
- 4Security is part of risk management and is reported to management.

**R3 · Business continuity**

- 1There is no plan for what to do if systems stop.
- 2We know which systems are critical, but the plan is untested.
- 3A recovery plan with time and data-loss targets, tested at least yearly.
- 4The plan is improved after every incident or test.

**R4 · Access and personal data**

- 1Access is shared; accounts of people who have left stay active.
- 2Access is granted on request, with no periodic review.
- 3Rights are role-based and reviewed periodically; personal data processing is documented.
- 4Access is monitored and unusual activity is detected automatically.

#### 8. Value and investment 3 criteria

**V1 · Business case**

- 1Purchases are made without assessing the benefit.
- 2The cost is assessed, but not the expected benefit.
- 3Every project has an expected benefit in money or time and an indicator to check it.
- 4Benefits are measured after go-live and shape the next decisions.

**V2 · Budget and funding**

- 1No budget; we spend as needed.
- 2There is an annual IT budget, mostly for maintenance.
- 3There is a development budget tied to the roadmap, including a check for funding programmes.
- 4Investments are managed as a portfolio with priorities and periodic review.

**V3 · Use of what has been implemented**

- 1Implemented systems are partly used or bypassed with Excel.
- 2We know which modules are unused, but nothing changes.
- 3Use of key functions is tracked and the reasons for workarounds are removed.
- 4Benefits are reported regularly and unused licences are cancelled.

## Methodological notes and limits

- Eurostat data was downloaded from the official API on 3 November 2026. Percentages are rounded to one decimal place.

- "SME" in Eurostat and Commission data means enterprises with 10–249 employees. Micro-enterprises are not included.

- The Commission states that 13.90% of SMEs sell online, while Eurostat's tables show 14.85% for web sales and 15.87% for all e-commerce sales (10–249 employees, 2025). We could not establish the reason for the difference, so we use Eurostat's tables.

- The Commission's cloud indicator (15.74%) differs from Eurostat's indicator for purchased cloud services (17.83% for 10+ employees, 2025). We use Eurostat's indicator.

- Export data by enterprise size is for 2024 and covers goods only.

- The 2023 edition of Deloitte Germany's Digital Maturity Index could not be opened; we cite the 2025 edition.

- We do not use sources we could not open.
